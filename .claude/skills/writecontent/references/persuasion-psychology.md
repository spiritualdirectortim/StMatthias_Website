## 1. Cialdini: the seven principles, diagnosed by blocker

Robert Cialdini (Arizona State; *Influence*, 1984 and 2021; *Pre-Suasion*, 2016) spent three
years undercover in sales, fundraising and advertising training to see which tactics kept
working. He groups the principles by the job they do. Use this as a diagnosis before you
write: what is actually stopping this reader?

| Reader's blocker | Principles to reach for |
|---|---|
| "I don't know or trust you" (relationship) | Reciprocity, Liking, Unity |
| "I'm not sure this is right" (uncertainty) | Authority, Social proof |
| "I'm not sure I'll actually do it" (action) | Commitment and consistency, Scarcity |

Pick the one or two that match. Stacking all seven in a paragraph is the amateur's tell.

### Reciprocity
- **Definition.** People give back the kind of thing they received first. Strongest when the
  gift is first, personalized and unexpected.
- **Study.** Restaurant mints: one mint with the bill, tips up 3%; two mints, up 14%; one
  mint, then the server turns back and adds "for you nice people, here's an extra one", up
  23%. The how mattered more than the what. Hotels: a sign saying the hotel had *already*
  donated to an environmental cause raised towel reuse 45%; promising to donate *if* guests
  reused did nothing. A handwritten sticky note on a survey returned 76% versus 36% with no
  note: personal, effortful, specific.
- **Moves.** Open a newsletter with something usable on its own (an answer, a prayer, a
  checklist) before the ask. Make the gift personal in the copy itself: "We saved you a
  seat" beats "Seats available." Sequence: gift, then ask, then nothing else competing.

### Commitment and consistency
- **Definition.** People act in line with what they have already said or done, especially
  commitments that are voluntary, active, public and effortful.
- **Study.** Almost no homeowners accepted a large ugly "Drive Safely" lawn sign, but four
  times as many did if ten days earlier they had put a small postcard in their window.
  Clinics cut missed appointments 18% by having the patient, not staff, write the card.
  Asking "Do you consider yourself a helpful person?" first lifted survey participation from
  29% to 77%.
- **Moves.** Ask for the small yes first ("Tell us which Mass you usually attend" before
  "Join the ministry"). Reflect the reader's prior act back: "You already come most Sundays.
  Here is the next step." Ask a question whose honest answer is the identity the ask will
  match, but only if the trait is genuinely theirs.

### Social proof
- **Definition.** Under uncertainty, people do what many *similar* others do. It works even
  on people who know it is happening.
- **Study.** Hotel towel cards: an environmental appeal got about 35%; "75% of our guests
  reuse their towels" raised reuse 26% over that; "75% of guests who stayed *in this room*"
  raised it 33%. Negative proof backfires: a sign saying "many past visitors have removed
  petrified wood" raised theft to about 8% from a 3% baseline; "the vast majority leave it
  untouched" cut it to under 2%. "Most of your neighbors conserve" produced 3.5 times the
  energy savings of any other message.
- **Moves.** Numbers plus similarity: "Join the 412 families who already give online", not
  "Many people give online." The closer the group, the stronger. Never describe the bad
  behavior as common; "too few people volunteer" teaches that not volunteering is normal.
  Specific, checkable testimonials from people like the reader beat star ratings.

### Authority
- **Definition.** People defer to expertise plus trustworthiness. Admitting a weakness before
  the strongest point raises trust.
- **Study.** Physiotherapists got more exercise compliance after hanging diplomas on the
  wall. Receptionists who introduced colleagues with credentials ("Sandra, who has over 15
  years letting properties here") produced 20% more appointments and 15% more signed
  contracts, even though the introducer obviously benefited.
- **Moves.** Put the credential in the sentence that introduces the person, not in a bio
  footer. Concede a small drawback first, then the strength ("It is a 90-minute commitment.
  It is also the thing people tell us changed their year."). Let a third party say it;
  self-praise reads as bragging.

### Liking
- **Definition.** People say yes to those they like; liking comes from similarity, sincere
  compliments and cooperation toward shared goals.
- **Study.** MBA negotiators told "time is money, get to business" reached agreement 55% of
  the time; those told to first exchange personal information and find one similarity, 90%,
  with deals worth 18% more. Cialdini's own landing page uses a welcome line, his signature
  in his handwriting and a photo, so "they see a person there".
- **Moves.** Lead with specific shared ground, not a claim about yourself. Sign as a person:
  "From Maria in the parish office" beats "From the Communications Team". Write "we are
  working on this together" frames.

### Scarcity
- **Definition.** People want more of what they can have less of. The engine is loss
  aversion and reactance. Newly scarce beats always scarce. "Loss is the ultimate form of
  scarcity."
- **Study.** When British Airways announced in 2003 that Concorde would stop flying, sales
  jumped the next day though nothing about the plane had changed.
- **Moves.** State the real limit in concrete units: "12 seats left", not "Limited spots!"
  Pair gain with loss: "Register by Friday and you keep the early rate; after that it is $40
  more." Use scarcity last, after value is established, and only when it is true. It is the
  easiest principle to counterfeit and the fastest way to lose a reader for good.

### Unity
- **Definition.** Not "like you" but "one of you": shared identity through kinship, place,
  faith, or acting together. "It's not liking, it's belonging."
- **Study.** Students offered a single extra-credit point if a parent completed a survey:
  parent response rose from under 20% to 97%. Asking colleagues for *advice* rather than
  their *opinion* turns a critic into a partner. A hospital sign "hand hygiene protects
  patients" raised soap use 45%; "protects you" did nothing.
- **Moves.** Use "we", "our" and "us" with a real referent ("our parish", "our block").
  Ask for advice, not feedback: "What would you tell a new family about this?" Name the
  identity the reader already holds before the ask, and invite shared action.

### The "because" study, read honestly
Langer's copy-machine study: "May I use the machine?" got 60% yes; adding "because I'm in a
rush" got 94%; adding the empty reason "because I have to make copies" got 93%. The half
usually omitted: when the request grew from 5 pages to 20, the empty reason collapsed to
the no-reason baseline (about 24%) while the real reason still helped (about 42%). Rule:
always give a reason, and the bigger the ask, the more the reason must be substantive.

## 2. Cialdini: Pre-Suasion, the moment before the message

- **Privileged moments.** Right after a well-placed question or cue, a person is unusually
  open to a matching request. Asking "Are you adventurous?" first raised contact-sharing
  from 33% to 76%. *Move:* open with a question the reader will answer "yes" to about
  themselves, then ask for the matching act.
- **Attention equals importance.** Whatever attention lands on is judged more important and
  more causal. A furniture site with cloud wallpaper made visitors rate comfort higher and
  buy comfortable sofas; penny wallpaper made them buy cheap ones; most denied the
  wallpaper mattered. *Move:* decide what the first sentence makes salient; it decides what
  the reader thinks the piece is about.
- **Channeled association.** Words send readers to areas of memory preloaded with
  associations. Calling crime a "beast" versus a "virus" shifted policy preference 22%,
  more than gender (9%) or party (8%). *Move:* choose metaphors deliberately; one word
  shifts a frame more than demographics do.
- **What holds attention.** The self-relevant ("you" beats "people"), the unfinished (an
  open question or half-told story), and the mysterious. Fear works only with a clear next
  step; without one it produces denial. *Move:* open a loop early and close it at the ask.
- **If-when-then.** Linking a cue to an action raised medication adherence from 55% to 79%.
  *Move:* put a cue in the call to action: "When you get home Sunday, text VOLUNTEER to…"

## 3. Cialdini: ethics (bunglers, smugglers, sleuths)

From his 1999 paper "Of tricks and tumors". The **bungler** fumbles a lever that is genuinely
there (forgets to mention he climbed El Capitan on the gear he sells). The **smuggler**
imports a principle that does not belong (fake scarcity, counterfeit authority, manufactured
consensus). The **sleuth** looks for which principles are truly present and surfaces them.
Cialdini's own account: "I wasn't exaggerating. I wasn't counterfeiting our connection: I
just pointed it out." His economic case: discovered deception cost Volkswagen fifteen
sixteenths of its sales relative to the industry after the emissions scandal, and people who
stay in dishonest environments cheat 77% more. "Those who cheat for you will cheat against
you."

Misuses that cheapen writing: fake urgency and invented counters; scarcity as the opener;
negative social proof; an empty "because" on a big ask; spray-and-pray stacking of levers;
"join the movement" with no real group behind it; self-praise where a third party should
speak; generic flattery ("valued customer").

## 4. Kahneman: the fast mind and what it does with words

Daniel Kahneman (1934 to 2024; Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics 2002 for work with Amos
Tversky; *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, 2011). System 1 is fast, automatic and always on;
System 2 is slow, effortful and lazy, and usually endorses what System 1 hands it. Copy is
read by System 1 first. The writer's job is to make the right thing the easy thing: easy to
read, picture, believe and do. Anything that wakes System 2 (jargon, ambiguity, a hard-to-
parse button) is friction, and a reader under friction becomes suspicious.

### Cognitive ease and fluency (the most writing-relevant chapter)
- **Finding.** Ease (familiarity, legibility, repetition, good mood) makes things feel true,
  liked and safe. "Familiarity is not easily distinguished from truth." Rhyming aphorisms
  are judged more insightful than the same thought unrhymed. Stocks with pronounceable
  tickers outperform. Hard-to-read fonts make people more careful and more accurate; easy
  fonts make a task seem faster.
- **Kahneman's own advice to persuaders.** "First maximize legibility." Use high contrast.
  "Do not use complex language where simpler language will do." Put ideas in verse if you
  can; they will be remembered and taken as truer. If you quote a source, pick one with an
  easy-to-pronounce name.
- **Moves.** Short words, short sentences, one idea per line. Repeat the key phrase (not the
  whole pitch) across the piece. Rhythm in headlines, sparingly, and only when the claim is
  true. Design is copy: legible type and plain layout are part of the argument. Use strain on
  purpose where you want careful reading (a consent line, a serious decision).

### Anchoring
- **Finding.** Any number considered first drags the estimate toward it, even when
  irrelevant; estimates typically move about half the distance to the anchor. "Powerful
  anchoring effects are found in decisions such as when people choose how much to
  contribute to a cause."
- **Moves.** Put the reference number first: "Most families give $50 a month; any amount
  helps", not "Any amount helps." In ask ladders, list the largest tier first. Show the full
  value before the discount.

### Availability
- **Finding.** Frequency and importance are judged by how easily examples come to mind;
  vivid, recent and emotional things feel common.
- **Moves.** One vivid, concrete case beats a statistic for felt importance; pair the story
  with the number. Ask the reader for one easy example, never a list ("Think of one person
  who would love this").

### Substitution and the affect heuristic
- **Finding.** Faced with a hard question, System 1 answers an easier one and does not
  notice. "Should I act on this?" becomes "Do I like these people?" or "How do I feel right
  now?"
- **Moves.** Know which easy question your reader will actually answer and write to it,
  honestly. Give a simple, true proxy: "If you have ever wished Sunday felt less rushed,
  this is for you."

### WYSIATI: what you see is all there is
- **Finding.** System 1 builds the most coherent story it can from what is in front of it
  and does not notice what is missing. Confidence tracks coherence, not evidence.
- **Moves.** What you lead with *is* the message; readers will not go looking in paragraph
  four. Three facts that fit together persuade more than ten that sprawl. Do not hide
  material caveats; when the reader later sees them, trust collapses.

### Prospect theory, loss aversion, framing
- **Finding.** Outcomes are judged against a reference point, and losses loom larger than
  gains, usually by 1.5 to 2.5 times (about 2:1 for money). The "Asian disease" problem:
  72% chose the sure option framed as "200 saved", 22% chose the identical option framed as
  "400 die". "90% survival" and "10% mortality" produce different choices, from physicians
  too. Organ-donation rates track the default box.
- **Moves.** Frame the status quo as the loss when you want action, and only when the loss
  is real: "Every month without a will is a month your family is unprotected." Frame the
  ask itself as a gain when you want it to feel safe ("you keep", "you get"). Set the
  reference point explicitly ("compared with last year's 30 volunteers"). Defaults are copy:
  pre-select the recommended option and say so.

### Endowment effect
- **Finding.** Once something is "mine", giving it up is a loss; owners demanded about
  $7 for a mug that buyers would pay $3 for.
- **Moves.** Write ownership before the ask: "your pew, your parish, your seat". "Reserve
  your spot" creates endowment; then frame cancellation honestly as a loss.

### Halo effect
- **Finding.** The first trait you learn colors every trait you do not observe. Order
  matters.
- **Moves.** The first attribute you give a person, program or place becomes the lens.
  Lead with the quality you most want generalized. Fix the typo in the first line before
  anything else.

### Narrative fallacy, overconfidence, planning fallacy
- **Finding.** We rebuild the past as a clean causal story, inflating skill and hiding
  luck. Confidence "is a feeling, which reflects the coherence of the information and the
  cognitive ease of processing it." Forecasts hug the best case; the cure is the outside
  view (base rates from similar cases).
- **Moves.** Do not sell one case as proof; say "here is one story" and give the base rate.
  Quote the reference class for promises ("most groups finish in six weeks"). Project
  confidence through clarity, not adjectives; readers correctly discount "amazing".

### Peak-end rule and the remembering self
- **Finding.** Memory of an episode is predicted by its most intense moment and its end;
  duration barely counts. Patients given a colonoscopy extended by a milder final minute
  rated it better and returned for follow-up more often. Subjects chose to repeat the
  *longer* cold-water trial because it ended slightly warmer. "The remembering self is a
  storyteller." "We don't choose between experiences, we choose between memories of
  experiences."
- **Moves.** Engineer one peak (a line, image or fact the reader will repeat) and a
  deliberate ending; the middle can be short. End on the strongest, most concrete note, not
  on fine print or a limp sign-off. Length adds nothing to memory, only to drop-off. Close
  a transactional email with the peak, not the admin.

### Focusing illusion
"Nothing in life is as important as you think it is while you are thinking about it."
Attention inflates importance (Cialdini builds Pre-Suasion on this). *Move:* put the
reader's attention on the attribute you want weighed, and nowhere else.

## 5. Kahneman: ethics and misuse

Kahneman presents the fluency tricks as the toolkit of "authoritarian institutions and
marketers", to help readers recognize them. He endorses nudging toward people's own
long-term interests while preserving choice: framing is unavoidable ("the tax table must be
framed one way or another"), so the obligation is to choose the frame that serves the
decider. On peak-end: "few patients will agree to expose themselves to pain for the sole
purpose of improving a future memory", so never trade the reader's real experience for a
nicer memory of it. And confidence is not evidence; do not manufacture it in copy.

Misuses: loss framing on everything until the reader habituates; obviously fake anchors
(crossed-out prices nobody paid); fluency mistaken for vagueness; rhyme attached to untrue
claims; vivid anecdote with no base rate, or a wall of numbers with no anecdote; burying
the one important thing below the fold; ending a strong piece on logistics or "Thanks!".

## 6. Cross-author synthesis (one screen)

1. **Decide the frame before the first word.** What you present first changes how the rest
   is experienced (Cialdini); what the reader sees is all there is (Kahneman).
2. **Make it easy.** Short words, legible, one idea per line, a key phrase repeated. Ease
   reads as truth; strain reads as risk.
3. **Make it concrete and local.** "412 families", "people who stayed in this room", one
   vivid case plus the number.
4. **Say what is lost, then what is gained.** Loss aversion (2:1) powers scarcity; use it
   only for real losses, late in the piece.
5. **Give before asking; ask small first.** Reciprocity and consistency build the ladder.
6. **Write as a person to a person.** Liking and unity: a name, a face, "we" with a real
   referent, ask for advice.
7. **Finish on the peak.** The ending is the memory; the memory is the decision to return.
8. **Be a sleuth, not a smuggler.** Surface the influence that is genuinely present.
