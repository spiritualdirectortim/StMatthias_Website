# Tactical empathy: Chris Voss on the page

Chris Voss led the FBI's international kidnapping negotiations, founded The Black Swan
Group, and wrote *Never Split the Difference* (2016). His frame: every attempt to get
someone to agree, act or feel satisfied is a negotiation, negotiation is emotional before
it is rational, and the goal is not "yes" but an understanding so complete that the other
person says "That's right." Most of his tools are spoken; this file translates them to
writing, where tone has to be carried by word choice alone. Examples are illustrations;
real copy uses only the project's facts.

## 1. The techniques, with written templates

### Tactical empathy
Deliberately understanding the other side's perspective, especially the unpleasant parts,
and saying it back calmly. Not sympathy, not agreement: "Tactical empathy does not mean you
agree with them or even like them." Feeling understood lowers the threat response and opens
people to influence; "empathy saves time" because it skips the argument stage.
- Open with their world, not yours: "You have been asked for money by this parish before,
  probably more than once."
- Name the unpleasant part first, with no denial and no "but": "It seems like the last
  change we made landed badly."
- Test every paragraph: could the reader say "that's right" to it? If it describes you,
  cut it or move it down.

### Mirroring
Repeat the last or most important one to three words the person said, as a question, then
stop. In Voss's first negotiation (a 1993 Brooklyn bank robbery) mirroring the robber's own
words kept him talking until he revealed his partners had left. It signals "I heard every
word", and the other person re-explains in new words, revealing more. It feels awkward to
the user; the other person almost never notices.
- Open a reply by quoting their phrase back: "'Nobody told us'? Tell me more about that."
  In writing, the question mark plus a line break is the pause.
- Mirror in a subject line to show the thread is about their words: "Re: 'too many emails'".
- Mirror one word to reopen a stalled thread instead of restating the pitch: "'Not right
  now'?"

### Labeling
Name the emotion or dynamic you sense, with hedge language that lets them disagree: "It
seems like… It sounds like… It looks like…" Then silence. Never start with "I" ("I'm
hearing that…" makes it about you). Naming a negative emotion diminishes it; naming a
positive one reinforces it. Voss talked three barricaded fugitives out of a Harlem
apartment over six hours using only labels in a calm voice.
- "It sounds like this came out of nowhere." / "It seems like you have been patient with us
  longer than you should have had to be."
- Label the dynamic, not only the feeling: "It looks like trust is the real issue, not the
  schedule."
- A positive label to lock in goodwill: "It sounds like this place matters to you more than
  you let on."

### The accusation audit
Before an ask or bad news, list the worst things the other side could be thinking about you,
plainly and without softening. Said first and stacked, the accusations sound exaggerated;
the reader pushes back on the exaggeration and is now invested in reassuring you. It must be
"done honestly, not as a matter of technique."
- Open a hard email with it, before any pleasantry: "You're probably going to think this is
  self-serving." / "This is going to sound like another ask for money."
- For bad news, the audit can be the subject line.
- Never follow it with "but", "however" or "that said". Those words erase the audit and
  turn it into a compliment sandwich.

### Calibrated questions
Open questions starting with "What" or "How", never "Why" (which reads as accusation). They
hand the other side the problem while keeping you in the conversation. "How am I supposed to
do that?" is Voss's "first and best way to say no." "The secret to gaining the upper hand is
giving the other side the illusion of control." "Yes is nothing without how."
- End a message with one calibrated question, not a demand: "What would make this worth
  your Sunday morning?" / "How would you like us to handle this next time?"
- Replace "Why didn't you…" with "What stopped this from working for you?"
- Test a soft yes with an implementation question: "How will we know this is on track?"

### No-oriented questions
Phrase the ask so the comfortable answer is "no": "Would it be ridiculous if…?" "Are you
against…?" "Is it a bad idea to…?" "Have you given up on…?" "Yes is commitment, no is
protection." People get defensive the moment someone fishes for a yes; after a "no" they
feel safe and in control, and they listen. Rewriting a fundraising phone script from a
yes-ladder to no-oriented questions roughly doubled donations.
- "Would it be ridiculous to schedule a 24-minute call next Tuesday?" (Black Swan's own
  template; odd, precise numbers read as considered.)
- "Is it a bad time to bring this up?" as the first line of a hard message.
- "Have you given up on [the project / coming back / the garden]?" as a one-line reopener.

### "That's right" versus "You're right"
Summarize their position and the feeling under it (summary = paraphrase + label) until they
say "That's right." "You're right" is a polite way of saying "please stop talking." In the
Philippines a kidnapper's $10 million "war damages" demand disappeared after a negotiator
summarized his grievances so well that he said "that's right" after nearly a minute of
silence.
- "So far, you have told me…" then a faithful recap of their points, including the ones
  that sting.
- Pair fact and feeling: "You asked three times and heard nothing, and it felt like nobody
  was home."
- Never write "You're right" as an opener; it reads as the same brush-off.

### The three voices and the late-night FM DJ voice
Positive and playful (the default, about 80% of the time, the "smiling voice"); late-night
FM DJ (slow, low, downward inflection, for bad news, firm statements, or when attacked);
assertive and direct (avoid; usually counterproductive). Calm is contagious; "the tone of
voice is five times as important as the words."
- On the page, the DJ voice is short declaratives, no exclamation marks, no hedging
  adverbs: "Here is where we are. Here is what happens next."
- The playful voice is light and warm, one small joke, one exclamation mark at most in five
  sentences.
- Black Swan's rule: read the draft aloud in the harshest tone you can imagine, because that
  is how it may be read.

### The 7-38-55 rule
About 7% of meaning comes from words, 38% from tone, 55% from body language. Writing has
only the 7%; the reader invents the rest from your word choice. So: use softeners ("I'm
afraid", "I'm worried") where tone would have carried them, an ellipsis as a written pause,
and move anything that matters to a conversation.

### Dynamic silence
After a label, mirror or number, say nothing and let them fill the space. In writing: one
idea, then a line break and nothing else. Do not stack three questions; ask one and stop.

### Loss aversion
People take more risk to avoid a loss than to secure an equal gain. In a Haitian kidnapping
the ransom fell from $150,000 to $4,751 once the kidnappers were reminded what they would
lose if the hostage was harmed. Precise numbers read as calculated and firm.
- "If this does not get fixed, the people who notice first will be the ones who already
  give the most."
- "Have you given up on…?" works because nobody wants to be the one who quit.

### The Ackerman model
Set the target; open at 65%, move to 85%, 95%, 100% in shrinking steps, each only after a
counter; end on an odd, precise number and add a non-monetary item to signal the limit.
- Ask for the small, odd, specific thing ("24 minutes", "two Sundays") rather than a
  round "some time." When a reader declines, step down to a smaller precise ask, not a
  repeat of the first.

### Black Swans
The small hidden things that change everything once known. Assume each side holds at least
three. When someone seems irrational they are misinformed, constrained or hiding an
interest. "If they're talking to you, you have leverage."
- Fish for the swan with one label plus one calibrated question: "It seems like something
  changed. What's different now?" Then move it to a conversation.
- Reuse the reader's own vocabulary and stated values back to them.

### The Rule of Three
Get the same commitment confirmed three times in three forms: a direct yes, a summary that
earns "that's right", and a how/what implementation question. "'I'll try' really means 'I
plan to fail.'"
- Across a page or email series: ask, recap in their words, then ask how or when it
  happens ("What day works?"). A confirmation note restates the plan and asks "What would
  get in the way?"

### "Fair"
Three uses: the defensive judo ("we just want what's fair"), the accusation ("we have given
you a fair offer"), and the honest proactive use, the only one Voss endorses: "I want you to
stop me the moment anything feels unfair."
- "If any of this feels unfair, tell me and I will fix it." Never write "to be fair" or "a
  fair ask" about your own position.

### Use your own name
Instead of overusing their name, humanize yourself with yours. People bargain harder with
institutions than with a person called Chris.
- Sign with a first name. "I'm Tim, and I'm the one who sends these" in a newsletter.

### The negotiation you don't know you're in
"The most dangerous negotiation is the one you don't know you're in." Tell-tale phrases:
"We have to…", "I need you to…", "You should…". Treat every page, reply and auto-response as
a negotiation. Skip the reflexive "I'm sorry", which Black Swan says helps about one time in
three; summarize the impact instead.

### The one-sentence email that gets a reply
When someone has gone silent: a single line, no pleasantries, no recap, no pitch. "Have you
given up on this project?" The reply is either renewed cooperation or a clean "no" that ends
the limbo. Send it one-to-one, from a person, after four to six weeks of silence; earlier,
or to a list, it reads as rude.

### Summarizing
Paraphrase (their content, your words) plus label (the feeling under it). Before disagreeing
in writing, summarize their position well enough that they would sign it.

### Three kinds of yes
Counterfeit (an escape hatch), confirmation (a reflex), commitment (the real one). Yes-
ladders train people to give counterfeit yeses. Never open with "Don't you want…?" Replace
"Ready to get started?" with "Is it a bad idea to…?" or a how-question.

## 2. Voss on written communication specifically

From The Black Swan Group's own pieces on email and chat.

1. **Do not negotiate by email if you can avoid it.** Email is for setting next steps,
   bookending a conversation, or recapping. Its best use is to get a verbal conversation.
   If they send paragraphs, reply with one sentence.
2. **Less is more.** "If your email exceeds five sentences, it's probably too long." One
   point per email so there is nothing else to argue with.
3. **Set it up, land it, finish positively.**
   - *Set it up*: a mini accusation audit, in the subject line for bad news. "This is going
     to sound horrible." / "You're probably going to be offended."
   - *Land it*: one calibrated or no-oriented question. "Would it be ridiculous to schedule
     a 24-minute call next Tuesday?"
   - *Finish positively* ("the last impression is the lasting impression"): a short,
     generous, forward-looking line. Never use the last line to win or to express dismay.
4. **Tone controls.** Softeners sparingly. Read aloud in the harshest voice. At most one
   exclamation mark in five sentences. Emojis simple and rare; a smiley can read as a smirk.
   In text, an ellipsis is dynamic silence.
5. **Frame "us-centered".** Tie the ask to what they already want.
6. **Opening a hard conversation in writing.** Accusation audit, label, stop. "You're going
   to think I'm a jerk for bringing this up. It seems like the last thing you need is one
   more email from us." Then the single question.
7. **Invite a reply, not compliance.** End with a question they can say "no" to or a
   what/how they get to answer; never "Please confirm by Friday."
8. **Silence is cheap in text.** In person, ignoring someone is an insult; over text it is
   normal. Design for it: short, one question, nothing they must defend.

## 3. Writing to frustrated, skeptical and lapsed readers (scripts)

Sequence: accusation audit, label (and summary until "that's right"), calibrated question,
no-oriented close. White space between moves. No "but".

**Frustrated** (a complaint, an angry reply):
1. Audit: "You're probably braced for an excuse."
2. Label and summary: "It sounds like you asked twice, heard nothing, and felt dismissed.
   That is on us."
3. Calibrated: "What would make this right?"
4. Close: "Would it be a bad idea for me to call you tomorrow around 10?"
Avoid: "We're sorry for any inconvenience", any "but", any defense.

**Skeptical** (a newcomer, a lapsed donor, a cynic):
1. Audit: "You have heard this before, and most of the time it was a pitch."
2. Label: "It seems like you would rather see what this looks like before anyone asks you
   for anything."
3. Calibrated: "What would you want to know first?"
4. Close: "Is it ridiculous to think one Sunday would tell you more than this page can?"
Add the proactive fairness line: "If anything here feels like a bait-and-switch, tell me."

**Lapsed** (gone quiet, stopped coming):
One line, no guilt, no recap, no-oriented and loss-framed: "Have you given up on us?" If
they reply, label what they say, ask one how-question, and move it to a conversation. Never
stack this with a newsletter or an ask; send it to the person, from a person, with a name.

For all three: write the reader's objections better than they would, one idea per message,
under five sentences, end on a generous note, read aloud in the harshest voice before
sending.

## 4. Misuses: when it reads as manipulative or robotic

- **Insincerity is the tell.** The audit "should be done honestly, not as a matter of
  technique." Mirroring "will feel unnatural if you're a phony." Intent leaks through word
  choice on the page as it does through tone in the room.
- **Robotic labels.** "I'm hearing that…" and "I understand…" shift attention to the writer
  and read as a customer-service script. A generic label ("It sounds like you're
  frustrated") is worse than none; make it specific to this reader.
- **Over-stacking.** One audit, one label, one question. Three labels in a row look like a
  template. Black Swan: "not everything you say will come from Black Swan techniques,
  because you are having a conversation as a human being."
- **The "but" erasure.** A label or audit followed by "but", "however" or "that said"
  cancels the move.
- **Fake no-questions.** "Would it be ridiculous if…?" followed by a hard sell is a trap in
  reverse; the reader must be genuinely free to say no.
- **Wrong timing.** "Have you given up on…?" before four to six weeks of silence, or sent to
  a list, reads as rude or passive-aggressive.
- **Mirrors in text without a question mark and a stop** look like mockery.
- **Awkward is normal; manipulative is not.** The test is whether the reader would feel
  fine seeing your notes.
