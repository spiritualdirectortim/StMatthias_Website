# Copywriting masters: Schwartz, Heath, Sugarman

Three books that turn psychology into sentences. Schwartz (1966) decides what the first
line may say. The Heaths (2007) decide what makes an idea survive the trip into another
head. Sugarman (2007) decides how to hold a reader line by line and how to edit. Details
marked "(book)" are relayed from summaries rather than a fetched primary source. Examples
in the "moves" are illustrations; real copy uses only the project's facts.

## 1. Eugene Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966)

Direct-response copywriter for Rodale and Boardroom; the book went out of print and sold
for hundreds of dollars a copy until Brian Kurtz republished it. His stance: copy is built
from blocks taken from the product and the market, not inspired. "Copy is not written.
Copy is assembled."

### Mass desire: channel, do not create
Copy cannot manufacture a want. It finds a desire already alive in many people, intensifies
it, and points it at one thing. Every product touches several desires; the headline may use
only one, and choosing it is the most important decision in the job. "Every product gives
you dozens of keys. But only one will fit the lock."
- Before writing, finish this sentence: "The reader already wants ___; this page shows them
  ___ is the way to get it." If you cannot, you are trying to create demand.
- Lead with the reader's existing want, not the organization's offer: "You have been
  meaning to ___. Here is the easiest way to start."
- One desire per page or email. Secondary desires go in the body, never the headline.

### The five levels of awareness
How much the reader already knows decides what the headline must do. The more aware, the
less you say.

| Level | Reader knows | Headline job |
|---|---|---|
| Most aware | Your thing, what it does, that they want it | Name it and the deal; little else. |
| Product aware | Your thing, but not convinced | Name it plus one new point: proof, mechanism, use. |
| Solution aware | They want the result; do not know you deliver it | Name the desire or its solution, prove it can be had, show the mechanism lives with you. |
| Problem aware | They feel the need; do not know a solution exists | Name the need and dramatize it; present your thing as the natural answer. |
| Unaware | Nothing, or will not admit it | No price, no name, no direct claim. Echo an emotion or identity that picks them out of the crowd. |

- Write the reader's level at the top of the draft. A visitor who searched your name is
  product-aware; a stranger from a shared link may be problem-aware.
- Most-aware template: "[Thing]. [Time, place]. [One line on how to get it]."
- Problem-aware template: "If ___ keeps happening, it is not your fault, and there is a fix."
- Unaware template, identity first: "For people who ___." / "You know that feeling when ___?"

### The five stages of market sophistication
How many similar claims the reader has already heard, and therefore how jaded they are.
Awareness is what they know; sophistication is how tired they are.

| Stage | Market | Response |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | First in the market | Simple, direct claim. "Be simple. Be direct. Above all, don't be fancy." |
| 2 | Competitors copying | Enlarge the claim: bigger, faster, surer. |
| 3 | Everyone claims the same | Introduce a mechanism: a new way the old promise works. The emphasis shifts from what to how. |
| 4 | Mechanisms compete | Enlarge the mechanism: easier, quicker, removes old limits. |
| 5 | The market disbelieves all claims | Drop the promise. Bring the reader in through identification and experience. |

Most web categories (churches, nonprofits, software, consultants) sit at stage three to
five. "The friendliest community in town" is a stage-one claim in a stage-five market and
gets skipped.
- Stage-three move, name the mechanism: "We do ___ by ___, which is why ___."
- Stage-five move, write to who the reader is and what it feels like: "This is a place for
  people who ___." / "Here is what a first visit is actually like: ___."

### The headline's single job
"Your headline's only job is to stop your prospect and get him to read the second
sentence." Judged on two requirements: interest and believability. Never echo competitors'
headlines; build yours from the product-market-moment relationship, which is unique.
Headlines are built piece by piece. Schwartz lists thirty-eight ways to strengthen a claim;
the ones that matter most on the web: measure it, compare it, make it sensory, put it in
action, state it as a paradox, remove a limitation, ask it as a question, name the problem,
show the before and after, address the reader directly, accuse the claim of being too good.
- Write twenty headlines by running one claim through patterns: "[claim] in [number]",
  "[claim] without [feared cost]", "How [specific person] [got claim]", "[Claim]? Here's why."
- Test a headline by asking only: would this reader read the next sentence?
- Prevention fails for the reader personally but works for loved ones: "for your kids"
  beats "for you" when selling prevention.

### Body copy technique 1: Intensification (the thirteen parts)
Present a series of fresh fulfillments of the one desire, changing the lens each time so it
never feels repeated. "Advertising is the literature of desire." In order: present the
satisfaction directly in detail; put the claim in action; bring in the reader (what the
first day is like); show them how to test the claim; stretch benefits out in time; bring in
an audience (what happened to others); show experts approving; compare and prove; picture
the black side, then solve it; show how easy it is; use metaphor; summarize; put the
guarantee to work and ask.
- Structure a program or about page as a descent: what it is, what your first Sunday is
  like, who else has done it, how easy it is to start, what to do now.
- "Bring in the reader" template: "The first week you ___, you will notice ___."
- "Test the claim" template: "Try this: ___. If ___ does not happen, ___."

### Technique 2: Identification (roles the reader wants to play)
People buy for a want and for what the purchase says about them. Character roles (a good
parent, devout, progressive) and achievement roles (homeowner, leader). "At least half of
all purchases cannot be understood in terms of function alone." Never contradict an accepted
image; fold a negative into a larger positive one.
- Name the role: "For parents who want ___ for their kids but will not ___."
- Offer the thing as acknowledgment, not instruction: "You already ___. This just makes it
  easier."

### Technique 3: Gradualization (a chain of accepted beliefs)
Belief is the reader's picture of the world plus the security it gives them. Never violate
it. Start from a statement they already accept and build a chain toward the claim they
would not otherwise accept. "One fully-believed promise has ten times the sales power of ten
partially-believed promises." The TV-repair ad opened "Was your TV purchased after the spring
of 1947?" (95% yes) and slipped "repairing a TV is so simple anyone can do it" inside a
claim already accepted. Devices: the inclusion question; the language of logic ("Here's
why", "Simply because", "Therefore"); the syllogism; "if… then"; paragraph parallelism.
- Open with a yes: "If you have ever ___, you know ___." Only then introduce the new.
- Follow every promise with a reason: "[Promise]. Here's why: ___."
- Hide the hardest claim inside a sentence the reader has already agreed with.

### Technique 4: Redefinition (remove the objection before it forms)
Rename or reframe so a roadblock disappears. Too complicated: simplify, then show the
simplicity. Not important enough: raise the stakes ("a $10 part could rob you of a $20,000
car"). Too expensive: inflate value, then price.
- Turn the flaw into the proof: "Yes, it takes an hour. That is exactly why ___."
- Escalate a small ask: "Ten minutes a week is the difference between ___ and ___."

### Technique 5: Mechanization (prove it works in one sentence)
Readers want image, then proof, then "how does it work?" Name the mechanism; describe it
("strong, quick promise, then the reason why you can deliver"); make it the headline when
the market is sophisticated. After the promise, one plain sentence: "It works because ___."
Use a mechanism the reader can picture, not jargon.

### Technique 6: Concentration (attack the method, not the rival)
"No successful copy ever sells a product. It sells a way of satisfying a particular desire."
Point out the weakness in the usual way, then show yours without it. "Most ___ do ___, which
leaves you ___. Ours ___." Only on a weakness you demonstrably lack.

### Technique 7: Camouflage (borrow the medium's authority)
Make the piece read like the medium's own content. Emails like a personal note, newsletters
like a magazine, not a flyer. Headlines as news or how-to; bylines; subheads as story beats.

### Momentum and the 33-minute habit
Transition phrases keep interest from flagging: "Here's how." "Let me explain." "And yet,
it's only the beginning." Plus deliberately incomplete statements answered later. Schwartz
wrote in timed blocks of 33 minutes and 33 seconds in which he could do nothing but ignore
the copy or work on it; a phrase would "ask" to be moved to the top. Stop mid-sentence so the
next block starts easily. Read the headline last and promote the best phrase from the body.

## 2. Chip and Dan Heath, *Made to Stick* (2007)

Chip Heath (Stanford) and Dan Heath (Duke). The framework is SUCCESs: Simple, Unexpected,
Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories. The villain is the Curse of Knowledge.

### The Curse of Knowledge
Once you know something you cannot imagine not knowing it, so you bury the lead, speak in
abstractions, and quote numbers nobody feels. Elizabeth Newton's 1990 Stanford study:
"tappers" tapped famous songs on a table and predicted listeners would recognize 50%.
Actual: 2.5%, three of 120. The tapper hears the orchestra; the listener hears knocking.
- Read the draft as someone who has never visited, never heard your acronyms, never met
  your staff. Replace every insider noun with the thing it is ("the narthex" becomes "the
  lobby").
- Test: could a new reader restate the one point after one read?

### Simple: find the core, then say it compactly
Not dumbing down; ruthless priority to one idea, then expressing it so compactly it travels.
"If you say three things, you don't say anything."
- *Commander's Intent* (US Army): the plain-language end state atop every order, because no
  plan survives contact. Found by finishing "If we do nothing else, we must ___."
- *Don't bury the lead*: Nora Ephron's journalism teacher gave facts about faculty traveling
  to a conference; every student wrote a who-what-where lead. The real lead: "There will be
  no school next Thursday."
- *Proverbs and names*: a small-town paper ran on "names, names, and names" and reached 112%
  household penetration.
- *Generative analogies*: "a pomelo is a supersized grapefruit"; "Die Hard on a bus"; Disney
  "cast members" (which implies onstage behavior) versus Subway "sandwich artists" (which
  implies nothing).
Moves: write the Commander's Intent for the page before the page and put it in the first
sentence. Borrow a schema the reader owns: "It's [familiar thing] for [new context]." Give
roles a generative label that implies behavior ("hosts", not "ushers").

### Unexpected: break a pattern, then fix it
Attention comes from surprise; sustained interest from curiosity gaps. Surprise must be
"post-dictable" (unforeseeable, then obvious) and aimed at the core, not a gimmick: the
Super Bowl ad where wolves ate a marching band was remembered and its product forgotten.
- A Southwest flight attendant opened the safety briefing with "If you haven't been in an
  automobile since 1965…" and got applause.
- *Nordstrom's "Nordies"*: new hires hear of the employee who gift-wrapped a purchase from
  another store and the one who warmed a customer's car. Common sense ("great service") does
  not stick; uncommon sense does.
- *Gap theory of curiosity* (George Loewenstein): curiosity is the itch of a hole in
  knowledge. The best science writing opens with a mystery (why did three teams get three
  answers about Saturn's rings?). People overestimate what they know, so make them guess
  before you reveal.
Moves: ask "what questions do I want the reader to ask?" rather than "what do I need to
convey?" Open a gap in line one: "Three things happen when you ___. The third surprised us."
Pick one counter-intuitive fact about the core and lead with it; drop the clever opener
that has nothing to do with the point.

### Concrete: paint with sensory detail
Something is concrete if the senses can examine it. "A V8 engine is concrete;
'high-performance' is abstract." Experts think in abstractions; the juror remembers the
toothbrush.
- *Aesop*: "sour grapes" has lasted 2,500 years; "don't be bitter when you fail" would not.
- *Velcro theory of memory*: memory is hooks and loops; a childhood home has thousands, a
  new credit card number has one.
- *Boeing 727*: not "the best plane" but 131 seats, Miami to New York nonstop, lands on
  runway 4-22 at La Guardia.
- *The Nature Conservancy*: instead of millions of unpicturable acres, named "landscapes"
  such as the Mount Hamilton Wilderness, so donors had something with a name to love.
- *Movie popcorn*: 37 grams of saturated fat is abstract; "more fat than a bacon-and-eggs
  breakfast, a Big Mac and fries, and a steak dinner, combined" is concrete.
Moves: replace every adjective of quality with a thing ("welcoming" becomes "someone will
learn your name by the second visit"). Give targets a picture: "[number] [people] [doing a
specific thing] by [date]." Name things; a named program, room or route becomes a place the
reader can go.

### Credible: let the idea carry its own credentials
When you cannot borrow authority, use antiauthorities, vivid details, human-scale numbers,
the Sinatra test, or testable credentials.
- *Antiauthority*: Pam Laffin, a 29-year-old mother with emphysema, starred in anti-smoking
  ads; credibility from first-hand truth, not status.
- *Vivid detail*: in a mock custody trial, an irrelevant detail (the boy's Darth Vader
  toothbrush) made the "diligent mother" side believable and swung verdicts.
- *Human-scale statistics*: an anti-nuclear group dropped one BB in a bucket (Hiroshima),
  then ten (one submarine), then poured in 5,000 (the arsenal). "Afterward there was always
  dead silence." "37% of employees" becomes a soccer team where four of eleven know which
  goal is theirs.
- *Sinatra test*: one credential that proves the rest. An Indian courier won a film studio's
  business by having delivered every Harry Potter book in India by 8 a.m. on release day.
- *Testable credentials*: "Where's the beef?"; "Are you better off than you were four years
  ago?" Let the audience run the test.
Moves: statistics as relationships ("one in every ___ households on your street"). Pick your
Sinatra example: "We handled ___. If we can do that, we can do ___." Offer a test the reader
can run this week: "Come once. If nobody says hello, email me and I will buy the coffee."
Let one person with no title vouch.

### Emotional: make people care
Not tear-jerking; making people feel something so they act. Levers: the power of one,
self-interest, identity, and retiring worn-out words.
- *Mother Teresa*: "If I look at the mass, I will never act. If I look at the one, I will."
  In a Carnegie Mellon study, statistics about African hunger raised $1.14 per person; the
  story of one seven-year-old, Rokia, raised $2.38; statistics plus Rokia fell to $1.43.
  Priming people with arithmetic cut giving: "the mere act of calculation reduces charity."
- *Semantic stretch*: "unique" rose 73% in newspaper use while losing its meaning.
- *Self-interest*: "People don't buy quarter-inch drill bits. They buy quarter-inch holes."
  Asking homeowners to imagine themselves subscribing lifted cable sign-ups from 20% to 47%.
- *Maslow's basement*: people assume others want cash while they themselves want
  recognition and meaning; aim higher than you would guess.
- *Identity*: "Don't Mess with Texas" cut litter 72% in five years by making litter
  un-Texan rather than un-tidy. Firefighters refused a safety film that came with a free
  popcorn popper.
Moves: one person, named, before any number. Write the "what's in it for you" in second
person and future tense: "You will leave knowing ___." Identity template: "People like you
don't ___. They ___." Retire exhausted words: unique, vibrant, authentic, passionate,
journey, impactful, community (as a noun for nothing in particular).

### Stories: simulation plus inspiration
A story is a flight simulator: hearing it fires much of what doing it would, so it rehearses
action. Stories arrive already concrete, emotional and unexpected; the writer's job is to
spot them, not invent them. "You can extract a moral from a story, but you can't extract a
story from a moral."
- *Three plots*: Challenge (an underdog overcomes; use to start hard work), Connection (a
  bridge across a gap, the Good Samaritan; use to build generosity), Creativity (a
  breakthrough; use to invite experiment).
- *Jared and Subway*: a franchisee spotted a student-paper story about a man who lost 245
  pounds eating sandwiches; national marketing had rejected the idea; sales rose 18%.
Moves: collect real stories and publish them close to verbatim with one detail only a real
person would mention. Keep it short: setting, obstacle, one specific turn, what changed. No
moral paragraph; the reader extracts it, and explaining the point turns the story back into
a claim.

## 3. Joseph Sugarman, *The Adweek Copywriting Handbook* (2007)

Mail-order entrepreneur who sold the first pocket calculator by mail, 250,000 "Pocket CB"
radios, and built BluBlocker sunglasses from a single 1986 ad. The handbook comes from his
seminars. He refined the advertorial.

### The axioms
1. **All the elements of an ad exist to get you to read the first sentence.** Headline,
   subhead, photo, caption, layout: one job.
2. **The sole purpose of the first sentence is to get you to read the second.** So keep it
   short: "Losing weight is not easy." "It's you against a computer." "It had to happen."
3. **The buying environment.** Layout, type, tone and the opening paragraphs set the mood in
   which the reader decides; a Tiffany box sells differently from a flea-market table.
4. **Resonate.** Get the reader nodding, feeling understood, before you ask anything. "The
   buyer and the seller must vibrate together."
5. **The slippery slide.** The reader should be unable to stop until the end: headline to
   subhead to first sentence to second, each pulling the next; subheads as eye relief and
   hooks.
6. **Seeds of curiosity.** End paragraphs with a short sentence that forces the next one:
   "But there's more." "Let me explain." "Now here comes the good part."
7. **Copy is emotion.** "You sell on emotion, but you justify a purchase with logic." People
   buy a Mercedes for status and justify it with the brakes.
8. **Never sell a product or service. Always sell a concept.** Not sunglasses but a "Vision
   Breakthrough"; not a walkie-talkie but a "Pocket CB".
9. **Incubation.** Load the brain with product and prospect, then walk away; the draft
   improves while you do something else.
10. **Length.** Copy should be long enough to cause the action you ask for. People read long
    copy about things they want. Price and complexity push length up.
11. **Personal communication.** Write to one person, "write the way you talk", use "I" and
    "you".
12. **Copy sequence.** Ideas flow as if answering the prospect's questions face to face.
13. **Copy is editing.** Express exactly what you want with the fewest words. Cut "very",
    redundant modifiers, throat-clearing openers. Read aloud. Sleep on it.
14. **Satisfaction conviction.** Beyond a guarantee: a statement so strong it says "I am so
    sure you will be satisfied that…" (keep the gift even if you return the product).
15. **A cure is easier to sell than a prevention**, unless the prevention is felt as a cure or
    the reader is buying it for someone they love.
16. **Assumed constraints.** Writers obey rules nobody set. Break the format when the concept
    demands it.

### The copy elements
Graphic: headline, subheadline, photo, caption, copy, paragraph headings, logo, price,
response device, layout. Captions are the second-most-read text on a page, so put a benefit
in them. Copy: first sentence, second sentence, product explanation, technical explanation
(builds authority even when skimmed), anticipate and resolve objections, clarity, no
clichés, rhythm (vary sentence length), where to get help, physical facts, trial period,
testimonials, offer summary, ease of acting, ask for the order.
- Raise the objection yourself before the reader does: "You may be thinking ___. Fair. Here
  is ___." Then resolve it in the next sentence.
- End every page with an explicit ask in plain words: "Come this Sunday at 10." Not "Learn
  more."

### The psychological triggers (use as an editing checklist)
Involvement and ownership; honesty ("the more honest you are, the more happy people will be
to deal with you"); integrity; credibility; value and proof of value; justify the purchase;
greed; authority; satisfaction conviction; nature of the product; nature of the prospect;
fads; timing; linking (relate the new to the known); consistency (small commitment first);
harmonize; desire to belong; desire to collect; curiosity; urgency (the enemy is "I'll think
about it", not "no"); fear (sparingly); instant gratification; exclusivity; simplicity;
human relationships (faces, hands, first person); storytelling; mental engagement (let the
reader reach the conclusion); guilt and reciprocity; specificity ("77.3%" beats "most");
familiarity; hope (never false). After the draft, tick the three the piece actually uses and
strengthen those; do not sprinkle all thirty-one.

### Famous examples
- **Magic Stat thermostat.** Opening: "You're probably expecting our typical sales pitch,
  but get ready for a shock." Then "When we first saw the MagicStat, we took one look at the
  name and said yuck." He knocked the name and the plastic case, then praised the one thing
  that mattered, installation so simple that "the red goes to the red, the blue goes to the
  blue", because the reader's real fear was hiring an electrician. The ad ran for about ten
  years. Admit the flaw first and the rest is believed.
- **BluBlocker, "Vision Breakthrough" (1986).** Subhead: "When I put on the pair of glasses
  what I saw I could not believe. Nor will you." Caption: "They look like sunglasses." First
  paragraph: "I am about to tell you a true story. If you believe me, you will be well
  rewarded. If you don't believe me, I will make it worth your while to change your mind.
  Let me explain." Then the discovery story, the mechanism, a testable challenge, a 30-day
  refund. "I don't think I could have sold it without putting in that story."
- **Clunkers he admits to:** a $1,500 laser mousetrap, a "nuclear mailbox". Failure is part
  of the method.

## 4. Cross-book working checklist

One job per line: Schwartz's headline rule and Sugarman's first-sentence rule are the same
rule. Each unit of copy exists to earn the next.

1. Reader's awareness level and the market's sophistication stage.
2. The one desire channeled and the one idea that must survive (Commander's Intent).
3. Opening line: short, yes-able, opens a gap.
4. One concrete person or detail before any number; numbers on a human scale.
5. Mechanism in one plain sentence ("it works because").
6. Objection raised and resolved; a flaw admitted.
7. Identity line ("for people who ___").
8. Plain ask with time and place; urgency only if real.
9. Cut every exhausted word; read aloud; does each sentence earn the next?

## 5. What makes copy feel like copy

- Creating desire from nothing: announcing a program nobody asked for with superlatives.
- Wrong awareness level: a discount code for a stranger; an explainer for someone ready to act.
- Stage-one claims in a stage-five market: "friendly", "welcoming", "vibrant", "the best".
- Burying the lead or saying three things.
- Curse-of-knowledge nouns: insider terms and program names with no picture attached.
- Semantic stretch: unique, authentic, passionate, excellence, journey, impactful.
- Statistics as output: "Over 2,000 served." Lead with the one, then the many.
- Gimmick surprise unrelated to the core; the reader remembers the joke and forgets you.
- Fake curiosity: gaps you do not close; "But wait!" with nothing behind it.
- Manufactured urgency and oversized fear.
- Claims unprepared by belief: jumping the chasm instead of building the bridge.
- Hiding the flaw: copy that admits nothing is distrusted.
- Feature lists in the voice of the institution: "We offer…"
- Trigger salad: every formula in one piece.
- Copied headlines: the breakthrough lives in this product, this market, this moment.
- No ask, or a timid one: "Learn more" where "Come Sunday at 10" is needed.
- Stories with a moral paragraph attached.
- Written in one sitting, never edited. First drafts feel like copy; third drafts feel like
  a person talking.
