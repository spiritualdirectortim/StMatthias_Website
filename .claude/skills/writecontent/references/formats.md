# Formats: patterns and rewrites

Each section gives the job of the format, the pattern that works, the budget, and a
before/after pair. The "after" lines are illustrations of the method; the names, numbers
and times in them are invented for teaching and must never be pasted into a real project.
Real copy uses only facts from the brief or the project's data.

## 1. Headlines and hero lines

**Job.** Stop the right reader and earn the first sentence (Schwartz, Sugarman). Not to
summarize, not to be clever, not to greet.

**Pattern.** Pick by awareness stage, then compress to 3 to 8 words with the key noun in
the first three. Schwartz's workable headline shapes:
- The offer, plainly (most-aware): "Christmas Eve Mass, 4 and 9."
- The specific proof (product-aware): "Forty-one winters on one roof."
- The mechanism (solution-aware): "Ten minutes a week, at your own table."
- The named problem (problem-aware): "Sunday mornings are a negotiation."
- The story or identity (unaware): "She came back after nineteen years."
- The question the reader is already asking: "What do I wear?" (only when the body
  answers it in the first line).

**Avoid.** Puns that cost a second of parsing, "Welcome to…", any headline that would fit
on every page of every site in the category ("A place to belong").

| Before | After | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Welcome to Our Parish Family! | Sit anywhere you like. | Most-sophisticated claim replaced by a mechanism of welcome; concrete; the wit is in the understatement. |
| Vibrant Faith Formation Opportunities for All Ages | Learning that starts with your questions. | Marketese out; concept (your questions are the agenda) in. |
| Support Our Important Roofing Campaign | The roof is 41 years old. | A fact the reader can weigh; the ask follows naturally. |
| Join Us for an Evening of Fellowship | Carols, cookies, cocoa. Saturday, 2:30. | Most-aware readers want the offer and the time. |

## 2. Subheadlines and decks

**Job.** Deliver the specific the headline withheld and start the slide.

**Pattern.** One sentence under 20 words. It answers "so what?" or "for whom?" and
contains one concrete noun. Never restate the headline in longer words.

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| *Headline:* The roof is 41 years old. *Deck:* Please consider supporting our parish's roofing fund campaign. | *Deck:* The Finance Council expects to replace it within five years, and we would rather start now than after the first leak. |
| *Headline:* Sit anywhere you like. *Deck:* We are a welcoming community. | *Deck:* Nobody has an assigned pew, kids make noise, and someone will say hello whether you want them to or not. |

## 3. Buttons and calls to action

**Job.** Say exactly what happens next, in the reader's words.

**Pattern.** Verb first, 1 to 4 words, names the destination or outcome: "Plan a visit",
"See Mass times", "Give to the roof fund", "Hold my seat", "Ask Fr. Roy". One primary
button per screen. The sentence before the button is the pre-suasion moment (Cialdini):
put the image or the small step there, not the logistics.

**Micro-copy under the button** reduces risk: "Takes about a minute." "No account needed."
"You can change this any time." Only when true.

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Learn More | See what a first visit is like |
| Submit | Send my question |
| Click here to donate | Give to the roof fund |
| Register Now! | Save a seat for Saturday |

## 4. Event blurbs and cards

**Job.** Let a scanner decide in three seconds whether this is for them.

**Pattern.** Metadata line (day, time, place) carries the logistics; the sentence carries
what it is, who it is for, and one concrete, slightly surprising detail. 15 to 40 words.
Cards in a grid should vary their first word, or the eye reads them as one block.

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Join us for a wonderful evening of Christmas caroling, cookies and hot cocoa with your parish family. All are welcome! | We walk the neighborhood singing, badly in places, then come back for cookies and cocoa. Bring kids, a coat, and no musical ability. |
| Rosary for Peace takes place on Thursdays at 10:00 a.m. in the church. All are welcome to attend. | Twenty minutes of the Rosary, prayed for peace, by whoever shows up. Thursdays at 10 in the church; sit near the front and someone will hand you beads. |

## 5. Section intros and page openers

**Job.** Orient the reader and promise the section's one idea, in 1 to 3 sentences.

**Pattern.** First sentence short and concrete. Second sentence the core. Optional third
sentence a seed of curiosity or the small step. The first paragraph of a draft is usually a
warm-up; delete it and check whether the second paragraph is the real opener.

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| St. Matthias offers a wide variety of ministries and opportunities for parishioners to get involved and share their gifts in service to the parish and the wider community. | Most people here started by doing one small thing once. Below are the small things. |

## 6. Email: subject lines and bodies

**Subject line.** Under 50 characters, specific, lower-pressure than you think. Schwartz's
stage rule applies: to a list that already knows you, the plain specific wins ("Caroling
Saturday, 2:30, meet at the church"). Curiosity subjects ("One thing before Sunday") work
once in a while and burn trust when overused. No "Newsletter", no "Update", no
exclamation mark.

**Body pattern** (under 150 words unless it is a letter):
1. First line: short, under eight words, and it carries the point or the small gift (an
   answer, a date, a thank-you with a detail). "Classes start September 14." The logistics
   that would make it long belong in the subject line or the second sentence.
2. One paragraph of why, with one concrete detail or one person.
3. The ask, as a sentence the reader can answer in a word. For a cold or lapsed reader,
   a no-oriented question (Voss): "Would it be a bad idea to hold two seats for you?"
4. What happens after they reply.
5. Sign with a name, not a department.

**Voss's one-line email** for someone who has gone silent: subject and body are the same
question, "Have you given up on [the thing]?" It gets replies because "no" is safe to say
and loss aversion does the rest. Use it once per thread, never as a template blast.

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| *Subject:* Important Parish Update! *Body:* Dear Parishioners, We hope this message finds you well. We are excited to announce that registration for Faith Formation is now open… | *Subject:* Faith formation starts Sept 14. Two weeks to register. *Body:* Hi Maria, Classes start Sunday, September 14, right after the 9:45 Mass, in the hall behind the church. Registration closes Friday the 5th because that is when we order the books. Would it be a terrible idea to put your two down now and sort the details later? Reply with their names and I will do the rest. Thanks, Anne |

## 7. Newsletters and bulletin notes

**Job.** Be read by people who did not plan to read it.

**Pattern.** One lead item with a real sentence, then short items each with a bolded first
few words that carry the noun. Each item ends on its next step, not its contact number.
One moment of wit per issue, usually in the lead or the last line. Thank specific people
for specific things (reciprocity and social proof in one move).

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| **Volunteers Needed.** We are still in need of volunteers for the hospitality ministry. Please consider giving of your time. Contact the office for details. | **Welcome Weekend needs two more donut people.** Eight already do it; two more means each of you works one Sunday a quarter. If you can pour coffee and say good morning, you are qualified. Reply to this note and Anne will put you on the list. |

## 8. Appeals: giving and volunteering

**Job.** Move one reader from agreement to a small, specific action.

**Pattern** (120 to 250 words):
1. Open on a concrete image or one person, not on the need. (Pre-suasion: the picture of
   the warm, dry church comes before the roof estimate.)
2. The honest situation, with one real number translated to human scale.
3. Admit the awkwardness or limit ("Yes, the roof. Again." or "It is not glamorous.").
4. The mechanism: exactly what the money or the hour does.
5. Social proof that is real and similar ("Sixty families gave last month; most gave
   between $25 and $100.") and, for giving, an anchor set by real suggested amounts.
6. One ask, framed by the honest loss if nobody acts, and the small first step.
7. End on the image again, or on the ask. Not on the mailing address.

What not to do: lead with the goal thermometer; use "prayerfully consider" as the verb;
pile on three needs; say how few people give; invent a deadline.

| Before (excerpt) | After (excerpt) |
|---|---|
| Our parish is in need of funds to replace the roof. The estimated cost is significant. Please prayerfully consider a donation to the Roofing Fund. Every gift helps. | The roof over the church is 41 years old, and it has not leaked yet. The Finance Council would like to keep it that way, which means replacing it before it fails rather than after a wet Christmas Eve. It is not a glamorous gift. Nobody names a shingle after you. But it is the gift that lets every other thing in this building keep happening. If a hundred households gave what they spend on one month of streaming services, the first phase is covered. |

## 9. About and "who we are" pages

**Job.** Answer "are these my people, and what would it be like to show up?"

**Pattern.** Open with the concrete, not the mission statement (the mission can come
second, quoted exactly). Then: who is here (real roles, real names where allowed), what a
first visit is like (sit anywhere, how long, what to wear, kids), one honest limit (we are
small; parking is tight), and the one next step. Staff and leadership listed with one
human detail each when available, not only titles.

## 10. FAQ answers

**Job.** Give the answer before the explanation.

**Pattern.** First sentence is the answer, as the reader would say it to a friend. Then at
most two sentences of nuance. Questions written in the reader's words ("Do I have to be
Catholic to come?"), not the institution's ("Participation requirements").

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| *Q: What should I wear?* A: St. Matthias welcomes everyone regardless of attire. Many parishioners dress in a range of styles, from casual to formal. | *Q: What should I wear?* A: Whatever you own. Jeans are normal here; so are jackets. Nobody will notice, and the one person who does is not someone you need to impress. |

## 11. Difficult messages: apologies, changes, bad news

**Job.** Keep trust while saying something unwelcome.

**Pattern** (Voss's accusation audit, then the facts, then the small next step):
1. Name the worst thing they are thinking, in their words, before they say it. "You were
   told 10 a.m. and showed up to a locked door. That is on us."
2. State what happened in one or two plain sentences. No passive voice ("mistakes were
   made"), no explanation that reads as excuse.
3. What is different now, concretely.
4. One calibrated question or one small offer. "What would make it right?" or "Would it
   help if I called you before the next one?"
5. A named person signs it.

## 12. Replies to upset, skeptical or silent people

Read `tactical-empathy.md` for the full approach. The short form, under 120 words:
- **Label** their state first ("It sounds like you have asked about this twice and heard
  nothing.").
- **Mirror** their key phrase once if it is doing work ("the same three families").
- **Do not defend** in the middle. Defense turns a conversation into a scoreboard.
- **State** the one true thing you can do.
- **Close** with a calibrated question ("What would make this feel handled?") or a
  no-oriented one ("Would it be unreasonable to ask for one more week?").
- **Tone**: late-night FM DJ. Calm, slow, downward. No exclamation marks, no "I'm so
  sorry you feel that way".

## 13. Social posts

**Job.** Stop a thumb, in the voice of a person.

**Pattern.** First line is the whole post for most readers: make it the detail or the
line with the turn. Under 40 words before the fold. One image described by a real alt
text. One link, named. No hashtags stacked at the end; one, if the platform needs it.

## 14. Talks and short speeches

**Job.** Leave one idea in the room.

**Pattern** (Heath): open with a story or an unexpected fact, not with thanks; state the
core once near the start and once at the end in the same words; one concrete example per
point; three points at most; end on the image or the ask, and then stop. Read aloud and
cut until it fits the time at a speaking pace of about 130 words a minute.
